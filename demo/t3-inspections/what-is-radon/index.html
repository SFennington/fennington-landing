Radon testing

What is radon, and should you test for it?

Radon is the second leading cause of lung cancer after smoking, and you can’t see or smell it. Testing is the only way to know a home’s level.

What is radon gas?

Radon is a colorless, odorless, radioactive gas. It forms naturally from the decay (breaking down) of radioactive elements, such as uranium, found in different amounts in soil and rock throughout the world. Radon in the soil and rock can move into the air and into underground water and surface water.

Why radon levels matter

Radon is the second leading cause of lung cancer, after smoking. Radon in a home is measured in picocuries per liter of air (pCi/L). The higher the level, the greater the health risk.

The US EPA and Surgeon General strongly recommend taking action when a home tests at 4.0 pCi/L or higher. Levels below 4.0 still pose some risk, and in many cases can be reduced. If the level is between 2.0 and 4.0 pCi/L, the EPA still recommends that you consider fixing the home.

The good news: radon can be fixed

Even homes with very high radon levels can be brought below 4.0 pCi/L, and many can be reduced to 2.0 pCi/L or less. Knowing the number before you buy lets you plan for it.

How T3 tests for radon

T3 Inspections uses a state-of-the-art digital continuous radon monitor. It runs a 48-hour test and takes a reading every hour, so you see how levels change over the whole test, not just one number. Once the test is complete the results are immediate, and in many cases the report is emailed the same day.

To fit the test around your inspection, Ted needs access to the home either 72 hours before or 48 hours after the inspection to set up or pick up the monitor.

Radon in well water

Radon can also dissolve into groundwater. If the home is on a private well, add a radon-in-water test to your inspection. It’s recommended for every home with a well water supply.
